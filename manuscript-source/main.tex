averaging for calibrated uncertainty\thanks{Preprint prepared for submission
to the International Journal of Machine Learning and Cybernetics.}}
\author{Chang Tan\thanks{Corresponding author: aquars43@foxmail.com}\,%
\thanks{Dalian University of Technology, Dalian, China.}}
\date{11 October 2026}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Practitioners who need calibrated predictive uncertainty usually
face a fixed training budget, yet method comparisons typically let every
method train until it stops improving. We ask how a fixed budget should be
spent. Under an identical budget of 60 SGD epochs we compared four allocation
strategies for a multilayer perceptron on Fashion-MNIST: one long-trained
network (\single); three independently restarted 20-epoch networks combined
by prediction averaging (\deepens); three cycle-end snapshots of one
cyclic-learning-rate run (\snapshot); and the uniform weight average of those
same snapshots (\swa). Across ten seeded replications, every strategy
improved test negative log-likelihood (NLL) over \single{} (Holm-adjusted
exact permutation $P=0.0098$), with the ordering \deepens{} $>$ \snapshot{}
$>$ \swa{} $>$ \single{} on every in-distribution metric. The ordering was
robust across budgets (30, 60, 120 epochs), ensemble sizes ($k=3$, $5$) and
a reversed dataset direction (MNIST in-distribution), although on the
near-saturated MNIST task only \deepens{} separated from \single{}
($\Delta$NLL $=-0.016$, adjusted $P=0.0098$). Only prediction averaging improved
out-of-distribution (OOD) detection, on both OOD probes and in both
directions; \swa{} showed no reliable improvement. Post-hoc temperature
scaling fitted per condition on validation data repaired much of the
in-distribution miscalibration of every condition yet left the budget-split
ensemble significantly best (NLL 0.289 versus 0.333 for calibrated \single{},
adjusted $P=0.0098$). The evidence is bounded to small networks and
grayscale image tasks, but within that regime it supports a simple rule:
split a fixed budget across independent restarts rather than extending
training or averaging weights.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Modern classifiers achieve high accuracy yet assign miscalibrated
confidence: networks trained for many epochs with small mini-batches become
systematically overconfident, so their predicted probabilities cannot be
interpreted at face value
\citep{guo2017,abdar2021}. Because downstream decisions, selective
prediction and abstention all consume these probabilities, miscalibration
is a deployment risk rather than a cosmetic defect, and a large body of work
now addresses uncertainty quantification in deep learning
\citep{abdar2021,ovadia2019}. Among the available remedies, three families
dominate empirical practice. Deep ensembles average the predictions of
independently trained networks and set a strong but compute-expensive
baseline \citep{lakshminarayanan2017,fort2019}. Single-trajectory
approximations reuse one training run: snapshot ensembles collect the
checkpoints left behind by cyclic learning-rate schedules
\citep{huang2017,loshchilov2017}, and stochastic weight averaging (\swa)
averages network weights along a (typically cyclic) trajectory
\citep{izmailov2018,wilson2020,maddox2019}. Variational and Bayesian
approximations such as Monte Carlo dropout form a third family
\citep{gal2016,welling2011}.

These families are usually compared at their own best operating points:
each method is tuned until it stops improving, and the reported trade-off is
accuracy or calibration against the \emph{added} cost of the method
\citep{ashukha2020}. Practitioners, however, face the inverse problem: a
fixed compute allocation and a choice of how to spend it. Under a budget of
$B$ epochs one may train one network for $B$ epochs, $k$ networks for
$B/k$ epochs each, one cyclic run of $B$ epochs whose cycle-end snapshots
form an ensemble, or the weight average of those snapshots. Benchmark
studies have mapped the performance of uncertainty methods under
distribution shift and documented the pitfalls of in-domain evaluation
\citep{ovadia2019,ashukha2020}, but they vary budgets across methods. The
question of which \emph{allocation} of a fixed budget buys the
best-calibrated uncertainty is therefore only partially answered, and the
mechanism behind the expected ordering---member quality versus member
diversity, prediction averaging versus weight averaging---is rarely
isolated within one controlled experiment.

We addressed this gap with a four-way, budget-matched, seed-paired
comparison in which every strategy consumed exactly $B$ epochs of the same
optimizer, architecture, data split and learning-rate ceiling. Two design
choices make the contrasts unusually clean. First, the first ensemble
member of the budget-split ensemble and the first cyclic snapshot are the
same network (verified bit-identical), so the \deepens{}--\snapshot{}
contrast isolates the effect of restarting training independently versus
continuing one trajectory. Second, \snapshot{} and \swa{} combine the
\emph{identical} checkpoints, so their contrast isolates the combination
mechanism---prediction space versus parameter space---with no trajectory
noise. The pre-registered core setting (Fashion-MNIST, $B=60$, $k=3$, ten
seeds) is extended here along four axes: total budget ($B \in
\{30,60,120\}$), ensemble size ($k \in \{3,5\}$), dataset direction
(MNIST in-distribution with Fashion-MNIST as the shift probe, in addition
to the reverse), OOD probe identity (MNIST and KMNIST), and a post-hoc
temperature-scaling baseline fitted per condition on validation data
\citep{guo2017,zhang2020}.

The contributions of this paper are ascriptive rather than algorithmic; we
offer a controlled measurement and a mechanism account, not a new method.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A compute-exact four-way comparison design} in which the
budget-split ensemble, the snapshot ensemble and the weight average share
training compute, data and (in part) checkpoints, with seed-paired exact
permutation inference throughout (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item \textbf{A robust ordering result.} Splitting a fixed budget across
independent restarts produced the best-calibrated uncertainty on every
in-distribution metric, and this ordering was stable across budgets,
ensemble sizes and both dataset directions tested
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-main}, \ref{sec:results-robust}).
\item \textbf{A boundary condition.} On the near-saturated MNIST task the
single-trajectory strategies stopped separating from the single long-trained
network, whereas the budget-split ensemble remained significantly better;
the ensemble effect in this regime requires headroom for longer training to
damage calibration (Sect.~\ref{sec:results-robust}).
\item \textbf{A separation of combination mechanisms under shift.} Averaging
predictions improved OOD detection on every probe--direction combination,
while averaging the same weights showed no reliable improvement
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ood}).
\item \textbf{A post-hoc recalibration stress test.} Temperature scaling
fitted per condition compressed in-distribution differences but did not
close them, and left OOD detection essentially unchanged
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ts}).
\end{itemize}

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the study
against prior work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} specifies the design,
metrics and statistical analysis. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
results organized by research question, Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
interpretation, limitations and practical guidance, and
Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Deep ensembles and their mechanism}
\citet{lakshminarayanan2017} showed that averaging the predictive
distributions of independently trained networks is a simple, scalable and
strongly competitive uncertainty estimator, and subsequent studies confirmed
its robustness under dataset shift \citep{ovadia2019}.
\citet{fort2019} analysed \emph{why} restarts help: independently
initialised networks converge to different basins of the loss landscape, so
their errors decorrelate. That analysis, however, treats ensembles as an
\emph{added} cost; the present study instead asks what restarts buy when
the budget is fixed and each member is correspondingly shorter.
\citet{ashukha2020} is closest in spirit: their ``pitfalls'' study of
in-domain uncertainty includes compute-matched single-versus-ensemble
comparisons and finds ensembles remain preferable. Our design differs in
two respects: we include the two single-trajectory strategies (snapshot
ensembles and \swa) that a budget-constrained practitioner would actually
choose between, and we hold checkpoints identical between the
prediction-averaging and weight-averaging conditions so that the combination
mechanism is isolated exactly.

\paragraph{Single-trajectory ensembles}
Snapshot ensembles exploit warm restarts \citep{loshchilov2017}: one
cyclic-learning-rate run yields several ``free'' models at the cycle minima
\citep{huang2017}. \swa{} instead averages the weights of checkpoints along
a trajectory and lands in flatter, wider regions associated with better
generalisation \citep{izmailov2018}; Bayesian readings and Gaussian
generalisations of the same idea include the work of
\citet{wilson2020} and \citet{maddox2019}. Related approximations reduce
ensemble cost by sharing parameters, e.g.\ BatchEnsemble
\citep{wen2020}. These works establish what each strategy achieves at its
own budget; the budget-matched trade-off among them, and in particular the
contrast between combining the same checkpoints in function space versus
parameter space, is what we measure. We also evaluate a denser \swa{}
variant that averages every epoch-end checkpoint, following standard \swa{}
practice \citep{izmailov2018}, as an exploratory bridge.

\paragraph{Calibration and post-hoc recalibration}
Overconfidence of modern networks and the expected calibration error were
popularised as practical metrics by \citet{guo2017}, who also introduced the
temperature-scaling (\ts) post-hoc fix we adopt: a single temperature fitted
on held-out data to minimise validation NLL. \citet{naeini2015} provide the
binning foundation for calibration measurement. Compositional post-hoc
calibration \citep{zhang2020} shows that calibration and ensembling
interact; the question we isolate is narrower and practical: does per-condition
\ts{} remove the advantage that budget-split restarts hold over longer
training, or does it merely compress the differences? Our setting extends
the classic observation that ensembles remain better calibrated than
recalibrated single models \citep{lakshminarayanan2017} to the
budget-matched regime.

\paragraph{Uncertainty under distribution shift}
The maximum-softmax score of \citet{hendrycks2017} is the canonical
baseline for detecting out-of-distribution inputs, and confidence-based
scores are known to be brittle \citep{nguyen2015}. \citet{ovadia2019}
benchmarked a broad set of uncertainty methods under shift, finding deep
ensembles the most robust; that study, however, varies training budgets
across methods and does not include the checkpoint-matched
snapshot-versus-\swa{} contrast that separates prediction averaging from
weight averaging here. Our OOD analysis uses two probes in each dataset
direction (MNIST and KMNIST against Fashion-MNIST in-distribution, and the
reverse), which lets us check that conclusions are not specific to one
probe.

\paragraph{Positioning}
No prior study that we are aware of isolates, within a single controlled
experiment, (i) the allocation of a fixed budget across restarts versus one
long run, (ii) the combination of identical checkpoints by prediction
averaging versus weight averaging, and (iii) the interaction of both with
post-hoc recalibration and OOD detection. We claim novelty for the
\emph{comparison design and measurement}, not for any of the component
methods, all of which are standard.

\section{Study design and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Budget-matched conditions}\label{sec:conditions}

The total training budget $B$ is fixed, and four allocation strategies
consume it in different ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}a). \single{}
trains one network for $B$ epochs under a single cosine anneal from learning
rate 0.1 to 0. \deepens (budget-split deep ensemble) trains $k$
independently initialised networks for $B/k$ epochs each, each under its own
complete cosine anneal, and averages their predictive distributions.
\snapshot trains one network for $B$ epochs under $k$ cosine
cycles \citep{loshchilov2017} and keeps the $k$ cycle-end checkpoints
\citep{huang2017}. \swa takes the uniform weight average of those
same $k$ checkpoints \citep{izmailov2018}. Throughout, the pre-registered
core setting is Fashion-MNIST with $B=60$ and $k=3$; the extension settings
vary $B \in \{30,120\}$, $k=5$, and the in-distribution dataset
(Sect.~\ref{sec:datasets}).

Two design details strengthen the comparisons. First, the first cosine
cycle of the cyclic run is, by construction, a complete $B/k$-epoch anneal
with a specified initialisation and data order; we verified
programmatically that this checkpoint is bit-identical to an independent
$B/k$-epoch run under the same seed, and used it as both the first
\snapshot{} member and the first \deepens{} member. The implementation is
therefore compute-exact ($2B + (k{-}1)B/k$ epochs per replicate) while the two
ensemble conditions differ, from the second member onward, precisely in
whether subsequent members restart independently or continue the
trajectory. Second, \snapshot{} and \swa{} are derived from the same run, so
their contrast measures only how the identical checkpoints are combined: by
averaging predictions (function space) or weights (parameter space). We
note that our \swa{} averages exactly $k$ cycle-end checkpoints in order to
remain checkpoint-matched with \snapshot{}; this is a restricted \swa{}
schedule relative to the many-checkpoint averages of standard practice
\citep{izmailov2018}, and an exploratory dense variant averaging every
epoch-end checkpoint is reported in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Datasets and splits}\label{sec:datasets}

Two dataset directions are used. In the primary direction, the
in-distribution task is Fashion-MNIST \citep{xiao2017} (60{,}000 training
and 10{,}000 test images, $28\times28$ grayscale, ten classes) and the OOD
probes are the MNIST \citep{lecun1998} and KMNIST \citep{clanuwat2018}
test sets (10{,}000 images each). In the reversed direction, MNIST is the
in-distribution task and the Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST test sets serve as
probes. KMNIST (handwritten cursive Japanese characters) provides a
second, visually closer shift probe whose label spaces do not overlap with
either in-distribution task. In each direction, the 60{,}000-image training
set was split once, with a fixed documented split seed, into 54{,}000
training and 6{,}000 validation images. Images were scaled to $[0,1]$ and
standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the corresponding
training split; OOD probes were standardised with the \emph{in-distribution}
statistics, matching the deployment situation in which a shift is not known
in advance. The validation split was used only for monitoring, learning
curves and temperature fitting (Sect.~\ref{sec:metrics}); no early
stopping, hyperparameter selection or model selection was performed on
validation or test data. The test set and the OOD probes were evaluated
exactly once per condition per replicate, after all training was complete.

\subsection{Architecture and training}\label{sec:training}

All networks were multilayer perceptrons with layers 784--128--64--10 and
ReLU activations ($\approx$110k parameters), without dropout or batch
normalisation. Training used mini-batch SGD with batch size 256 (211 steps
per epoch on 54{,}000 images), momentum 0.9 and weight decay $10^{-4}$
(coupled). Learning rates followed per-step cosine schedules from
$\eta_{\max}=0.1$ to $0$: a single $B$-epoch anneal for \single{}, $k$
cycles for the cyclic run, and one $B/k$-epoch anneal per \deepens{}
member. Initialisation used PyTorch's default Kaiming-uniform scheme; the
data order of each run was generated from a dedicated seeded generator.
Hyperparameters were fixed from common practice before any test-set
evaluation, and a pilot confirmed training stability using training and
validation losses only. On two CPU cores, a $B=60$ replicate took
72--87\,s of wall-clock time; the extension settings required about 9,000
training epochs (69 minutes of wall-clock time), and together with the
pre-registered core and the supplementary dense-\swa{} pass the complete
study comprises roughly 11,200 epochs, under two hours of single-machine
CPU time.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

All metrics were computed on the full test set (and, for OOD, the full
probe) with probabilities evaluated in float64. NLL is the mean negative
log-probability of the true class under the (ensemble-mean) predictive
distribution. Accuracy is the fraction of argmax-correct predictions. The
Brier score is the mean over samples of the summed squared error between
the predictive distribution and the one-hot label. Expected calibration
error (ECE) uses 15 equal-width bins over maximum predicted probability
\citep{naeini2015,guo2017}, with 10 bins as a sensitivity check. OOD
detection uses the AUROC of the maximum-softmax score
\citep{hendrycks2017} with in-distribution samples as the positive class,
and the negative-entropy score as a sensitivity check. Ensemble disagreement
is the mean over test samples and member pairs of the Jensen--Shannon
divergence between member predictive distributions (natural logarithm).
Ensemble predictive distributions are the arithmetic mean of member softmax
outputs.

\paragraph{Temperature scaling}
Following \citet{guo2017}, we fit a single temperature $T$ per condition
and replicate by minimising NLL on the 6{,}000 validation images, and apply
it to the test and probe sets. The scaled predictive distribution is
$\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)$ where $z$ is the effective logit vector of the
condition: the raw logits for single-model conditions, and the logarithm of
the averaged probabilities for ensemble conditions (an averaged ensemble
has no raw logits of its own). For single networks this coincides exactly
with standard temperature scaling, because the softmax is shift-invariant
and $\log p$ differs from the raw logits only by a constant per sample.
The fitted temperature itself is reported as an overconfidence diagnostic.
This per-condition \ts{} is the strongest simple post-hoc recalibration
baseline \citep{guo2017,zhang2020}: it asks whether the between-strategy
differences survive when every strategy is allowed its own best
recalibration.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}

The analysis of the core setting (including the contrast set, the primary
outcome and the multiplicity strategy) was written and stored before any
test-set evaluation; the only amendment (increasing replications from five
to ten) was made after a timing pilot, before any outcome was observed.
The independent experimental unit is the seed; each seed contributes one
value per condition per metric, and all contrasts are within-seed paired
differences. We report mean differences with two-sided 95\% $t$-intervals
($n-1=9$ degrees of freedom), exact two-sided sign-flip permutation $P$
values enumerating all $2^{10}$ sign patterns, and Holm--Bonferroni
adjustment across the five pre-specified contrasts applied to the primary
outcome (NLL). The five contrasts are \deepens$-$\single,
\snapshot$-$\single, \swa$-$\single, \deepens$-$\snapshot{} and
\snapshot$-$\swa{}; the first three measure what each strategy buys over
spending the whole budget on one network, and the last two locate the
ordering. The extension settings ($B$, $k$, dataset direction, probes) and
the temperature-scaled analyses were not part of the pre-registered plan
and are labelled as such; each extension setting is analysed with the same
machinery and its own Holm family over the same five contrasts on NLL,
and the \ts{} family on \ts-NLL is likewise Holm-corrected. Sensitivity
analyses (10-bin ECE, entropy-based AUROC score, dense \swa{}) were
specified in advance or, for dense \swa{}, labelled exploratory. No data
were excluded; no condition, replicate or sample was dropped at any stage.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

Experiments used PyTorch 2.14.0 (CPU), NumPy 2.1.3, SciPy 1.14.1 and
scikit-learn 1.5.2 on Python 3.12 (two CPU cores, no GPU). Every training
run is deterministic given its replicate seed; the v2 pipeline used for the
extension settings reproduces the pre-registered v1 records exactly (a
seed-0 re-run matched all 28 recorded metrics bit-for-bit, and the shared
first member passed bit-identity checks in both pipelines). Training,
analysis and figure scripts, the raw per-replicate metric records
(JSONL), the aggregated analysis outputs and the per-panel figure source
data are released in a public repository
(\url{https://github.com/43aquarius/IJMLC}); datasets are publicly
available \citep{xiao2017,lecun1998,clanuwat2018}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Budget-matched comparison in the core setting}
\label{sec:results-main}

All three ensembling strategies improved the primary outcome, test NLL,
over \single{} at matched budget (Table~\ref{tab:contrasts},
Fig.~\ref{fig:main}b): mean paired differences were $-0.2245$ (95\% CI
$[-0.2360,-0.2130]$) for \deepens{}, $-0.1780$ for \snapshot{} and
$-0.1396$ for \swa{}, each with exact permutation $P=0.0020$ and
Holm-adjusted $P=0.0098$ across the five pre-specified contrasts. The
strategies were also strictly ordered: \deepens{} improved on \snapshot{}
by $-0.0465$ ($[-0.0505,-0.0425]$) and \snapshot{} improved on \swa{} by
$-0.0384$ ($[-0.0407,-0.0360]$), both with adjusted $P=0.0098$. The
single long-trained network combined the highest per-epoch accuracy
trajectory with the worst predictive log-likelihood: its NLL of $0.5265
\pm 0.0159$ (mean $\pm$ s.d.) coexisted with accuracy of $0.8895 \pm
0.0015$, reflecting severe overconfidence rather than classification
error.

The same ordering held on every secondary in-distribution metric
(Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). Accuracy improved modestly but consistently,
the Brier score and ECE followed the NLL ordering, and the effect sizes
were stable across replicates (all ten replicates favoured every strategy
over \single{} for NLL). The reliability diagrams locate the differences
in the confidence scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}c, Supplementary Fig.~S1): the
single network's predictions clustered at high confidence with accuracy
below that confidence (ECE $0.0702 \pm 0.0021$; a typical predicted
confidence of about $0.96$ was matched by roughly $0.89$ empirical
accuracy), \deepens{} nearly restored calibration (ECE $0.0236 \pm
0.0008$, a 66\% reduction), \snapshot{} removed about a third of the error
and \swa{} about a quarter. These calibration gains, rather than accuracy,
carried most of the NLL improvement.

\subsection{Robustness across budgets, ensemble sizes and dataset direction}
\label{sec:results-robust}

The extension settings test whether the core ordering is an artefact of
one budget, one ensemble size or one dataset direction
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}, Table~\ref{tab:extension}). Across budgets
$B \in \{30, 60, 120\}$ the NLL ordering \deepens{} $>$ \snapshot{} $>$
\swa{} $>$ \single{} was preserved, with all five contrasts
Holm-adjusted $P=0.0098$ at every budget. The budget-split advantage
\emph{grew} with the budget: the \deepens$-$\single{} difference widened
from $-0.0754$ at $B=30$ to $-0.2245$ at $B=60$ and $-0.3317$ at $B=120$,
because longer training made the single network progressively more
overconfident (its NLL rose from $0.3795$ to $0.5265$ to $0.6672$ while
its accuracy was flat) while the 20- or 40-epoch restart members stayed
well calibrated. Enlarging the ensemble from $k=3$ to $k=5$ at fixed
budget improved every strategy (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}b) and preserved the
ordering (\deepens{} $-0.2290$ vs.\ \single{}, adjusted $P=0.0098$).

Reversing the dataset direction exposed a boundary condition. With MNIST
as the in-distribution task (accuracy $0.982$ for \single{}; ECE $0.0098$),
only \deepens{} separated from \single{} on NLL ($-0.0158$, 95\% CI
$[-0.0183,-0.0134]$, adjusted $P=0.0098$); the single-trajectory
strategies showed no reliable improvement (\snapshot{} $-0.0015$,
$P=0.41$; \swa{} $+0.0017$, $P=0.41$; Fig.~\ref{fig:direction}a). The
ensemble effect that longer training damages calibration and averaging
repairs it---requires headroom that the near-saturated MNIST task does not
provide: the single 60-epoch network was already well calibrated there
(ECE $0.0098$ versus $0.0702$ on Fashion-MNIST). The budget-split restarts
nevertheless remained the best allocation even on MNIST: \deepens{} still
beat \snapshot{} ($-0.0143$, adjusted $P=0.0098$), and by adjacency also
\swa{} (the \snapshot$-$\swa{} contrast was itself significant at adjusted
$P=0.0098$).

\subsection{Post-hoc temperature scaling compresses but does not close the
gaps}\label{sec:results-ts}

A natural objection is that the between-strategy differences in NLL reflect
fixable overconfidence rather than information content: a single temperature
fitted on validation data repairs much of a network's miscalibration at
negligible cost \citep{guo2017}. We therefore fitted one temperature per
condition and replicate (Fig.~\ref{fig:ts}) and re-evaluated all metrics.
Temperature scaling behaved as expected: it helped every condition, and
most dramatically the single network, whose NLL fell from $0.5265$ to
$0.3325$ and whose ECE fell from $0.0702$ to $0.0111$. The fitted
temperatures mirror the overconfidence ordering (\single{} $2.46$,
\swa{} $1.79$, \snapshot{} $1.58$, \deepens{} $1.25$): the strategies that
looked worst calibrated uncalibrated needed the most cooling.

Two findings survive the recalibration, however. First, the ordering
persisted: after per-condition \ts{}, \deepens{} still attained the lowest
NLL ($0.2891 \pm 0.0020$) and every contrast in the five-contrast family
remained significant at Holm-adjusted $P=0.0098$ (\deepens$-$\single{}
$-0.0434$, 95\% CI $[-0.0468,-0.0400]$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ts}a). Budget-split
restarts therefore do not merely produce a conveniently recalibratable
distribution; averaging independent restarts yields information that a
monotone rescaling of any single trajectory cannot recover. Second,
recalibration bought nothing where the deployment risk lives: \ts{}
changed OOD AUROC by at most $+0.012$ on any condition or probe
(e.g.\ \single{} $0.7533 \rightarrow 0.7620$ on the MNIST probe,
\deepens{} $0.8502 \rightarrow 0.8566$), leaving the between-strategy OOD
differences intact (Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ood}). The same two conclusions
held in every extension setting: after \ts{}, \deepens{} remained
significantly better than \single{} on NLL at $B=30$ ($-0.0114$),
$B=120$ ($-0.0567$), $k=5$ ($-0.0384$) and in the MNIST direction
($-0.0071$), all adjusted $P=0.0098$. In the low-budget setting the
\ts-adjusted strategies were close together (NLL $0.299$--$0.313$), so
post-hoc calibration is a genuine partial substitute when the budget is
small; at and beyond the core budget it is not.

\subsection{OOD detection separates prediction averaging from weight
averaging}\label{sec:results-ood}

We next asked which budget allocations buy robustness, measured as the
separation between in-distribution test images and each OOD probe using
the maximum-softmax score (Fig.~\ref{fig:direction}b). In the core
setting, \deepens{} improved AUROC by $+0.0969$ over \single{} on the MNIST
probe (95\% CI $[+0.0847,+0.1091]$, $P=0.0020$) and by $+0.1097$
($[+0.1004,+0.1190]$) on the KMNIST probe, whereas \swa{} showed no
reliable improvement on the MNIST probe ($-0.0029$, $[-0.0166,+0.0109]$,
$P=0.64$) and only a small, order-of-magnitude-smaller effect on KMNIST
($+0.0112$, $[+0.0023,+0.0202]$, $P=0.023$ uncorrected). \snapshot{}
occupied an intermediate position ($+0.0423$ and $+0.0585$
respectively). Because \snapshot{} and \swa{} combine the identical three
checkpoints, this difference is attributable to the combination mechanism
alone: averaging predictions transfers the members' ability to disagree on
shifted inputs into the score, while averaging weights collapses the
members into one network whose confidence behaviour under shift is
essentially that of the trajectory it came from. We note that the width of
the \swa{} interval (about $0.028$ AUROC units) bounds what this design can
resolve; the claim is one of absence of evidence at this precision, not of
evidence of absence.

The pattern generalised across probes and directions. Across the four
combinations of in-distribution task and OOD probe, \deepens{} gave the
best OOD detection and was the only strategy whose
improvement over \single{} was significant on every combination (in the
MNIST direction: $+0.0139$, $P=0.008$, and $+0.0235$, $P=0.002$); the
single-trajectory strategies were statistically indistinguishable from
\single{} on both probes of the MNIST direction ($P \geq 0.08$). The same
pattern held with the negative-entropy score, so the conclusion is not
specific to the score function, and the exploratory dense \swa{} variant
(40-checkpoint average) likewise left OOD detection unchanged
(Supplementary Table~S2).

\subsection{Mechanism: member calibration and member diversity}
\label{sec:results-mech}

The design also pins down why \deepens{} dominates \snapshot{} at matched
budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}e,f); because the two ensembles share their
first member, this contrast is carried by the later members, where the
strategies differ in restarting versus continuing training. First, the
individual members differed in calibration: the three independent 20-epoch
restarts achieved a mean member NLL of $0.3379 \pm 0.0022$, whereas the
second and third cyclic snapshots (taken after 40 and 60 cumulative epochs
of continued training) were individually more overconfident ($0.4039 \pm
0.0087$ averaged over members), even though their member accuracy was
nearly identical ($0.8912$ versus $0.8916$). Continued training on the
same trajectory thus bought no member-level accuracy but cost
member-level calibration. Second, the members were less diverse: the mean
pairwise Jensen--Shannon divergence of member predictive distributions was
$0.0150 \pm 0.0003$ nats for \deepens{} but only $0.0106 \pm 0.0003$ nats
for \snapshot{}, a 30\% reduction in disagreement. At $k=5$ the same two
ingredients pointed the same way (member NLL $0.3212$ versus $0.3796$; JSD
$0.0101$ versus $0.0107$, the diversity gap narrowing as the shorter
12-epoch members become more similar). Both quantities point in the
direction that favours independent restarts; we note, however, that with
$n=10$ replicates and $k \leq 5$ members these measurements associate the
mechanism with the outcome rather than establishing it causally.

% ─────────────────────────────── 5 discussion ───────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

At a fixed training budget, this study returns a consistent allocation
rule: splitting the budget across independent restarts (\deepens{})
produced the most accurate and best-calibrated uncertainty in every
setting we measured---both dataset directions, three budgets spanning a
$4\times$ range, and two ensemble sizes; a cyclic-schedule snapshot
ensemble (\snapshot{}) captured most of the in-distribution benefit at one
run's bookkeeping cost; and weight averaging (\swa{}) improved
in-distribution metrics but, unlike either prediction-averaging strategy,
showed no reliable improvement in OOD detection. Because the four
conditions shared data, architecture, optimizer, budget and pairing by
seed, and because \snapshot{} and \swa{} shared the identical checkpoints,
these differences are attributable to the allocation of the budget and the
mechanism of combination rather than to tuning or trajectory noise.

The mechanism measurements suggest an interpretation in terms of where the
two averaging operations act. Prediction averaging operates in function
space: when members disagree, the mixture flattens the predictive
distribution, which is exactly the behaviour that repairs overconfidence
and that produces low-confidence predictions on shifted inputs. Weight
averaging operates in parameter space: it selects a flatter, lower-loss
point along the trajectory \citep{izmailov2018,wilson2020}, which improves
the network's fit--confidence trade-off in distribution, but it collapses
the members' disagreement into a single confident model and thereby
discards the epistemic signal that the shift detector consumes. Consistent
with this reading, member-level analysis showed that continued-training
snapshots are individually more overconfident than fresh restarts at equal
accuracy, and less diverse from one another; the snapshot ensemble
therefore starts from worse-calibrated, more correlated members than the
budget-split ensemble even at the same budget.

The budget sweep adds a time dimension to this account. Longer training of
the single network made it progressively more overconfident---its NLL
\emph{worsened} from $0.38$ at $B=30$ to $0.67$ at $B=120$ while accuracy
was flat---so the value of redirecting that budget into restarts grew
with the budget. The reversed dataset direction shows the complementary
boundary: on a near-saturated task where the long-trained single network
is already well calibrated, the single-trajectory strategies had nothing
left to repair and stopped separating from \single{}, while budget-split
restarts still helped. The ensemble effect documented here is therefore
not universal; it is a property of regimes with calibration headroom,
which include most practically deployed under-trained or heavily optimised
classifiers and exclude easy saturated tasks.

Post-hoc recalibration is the strongest available objection to any
calibration comparison, and our answer is two-sided. In distribution,
per-condition temperature scaling is a genuine partial substitute,
especially at small budgets where the \ts-adjusted strategies clustered
within $0.014$ NLL of each other; at the core budget and above, however,
the recalibrated budget-split ensemble remained significantly better than
every recalibrated alternative. Under shift, temperature scaling bought
nothing---it changes confidence levels, not their ranking across
inputs---so the OOD advantage of prediction averaging cannot be recovered
post hoc. This refines the classical observation that ensembles remain
better calibrated than recalibrated single models
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017,guo2017,zhang2020}: in the budget-matched
regime, what post-hoc calibration cannot buy is precisely the
restart-induced diversity.

Our results are consistent with the literature that motivated the
comparison---deep ensembles are strong but assumed expensive
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017,fort2019}, snapshot ensembles are attractive
because one run yields several models \citep{huang2017}, and \swa{}
improves accuracy and in-distribution calibration
\citep{izmailov2018,wilson2020}---but they quantify the trade-off under
the constraint that matters in practice. The budget-matched framing
reverses the usual cost accounting: a deep ensemble of three 20-epoch runs
is not three times as expensive as one 60-epoch run; it \emph{is} the same
budget spent differently. Under that accounting, the ``expensive''
strategy was uniformly best in this regime, the ``free'' strategies
captured different fractions of its benefit, and the cheapest-to-deploy
single-trajectory variant (\swa{}) bought no shift robustness. This
complements benchmark studies that vary budgets across methods
\citep{ovadia2019,ashukha2020} and echoes the observation that
independent initialisation is the ingredient most responsible for
ensemble gains \citep{fort2019}: here that ingredient survives a
three-fold reduction in per-member training.

For practitioners, the allocation rule we can support is as follows. At
small scale and fixed compute, prefer independent restarts over longer
training for calibrated uncertainty. If only a single trajectory is
affordable, prefer its snapshot ensemble over its weight average when
shift robustness matters, and apply temperature scaling regardless: it is
nearly free, repairs in-distribution overconfidence, and does no harm to
shift detection. Treat \swa{} as an accuracy-and-fit tool rather than a
shift detector, consistent with evidence that OOD failures often persist
across training procedures \citep{hendrycks2017,nguyen2015}. The rule is
bounded by the scope of the evidence, which we summarise in
Sect.~\ref{sec:limitations}.

% ─────────────────────────────── 6 limitations ───────────────────────────────
\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Our study used one architecture family (a small multilayer perceptron
without batch normalisation or dropout); convolutional and
transformer-based networks, and larger models in general, remain untested
here, and the CPU-bound design is the reason. The dataset families are two
grayscale $28 \times 28$ image tasks and their probes; conclusions about
natural-image or cross-modal shift would require further experiments. The
budget is denominated in epochs at a fixed learning-rate ceiling and
optimizer family rather than wall-clock or energy; other optimizers or
schedules could shift the crossover points, although not the
checkpoint-matched logic of the \snapshot$-$\swa{} contrast. Ensemble
sizes are $k \in \{3,5\}$. Our \swa{} variant is checkpoint-matched to
the snapshot ensemble by design; although a dense 40-checkpoint average
did not change the qualitative conclusion, other \swa{} schedules might.
Temperature scaling is the only post-hoc recalibration tested; more
flexible calibrators \citep{zhang2020} could in principle compress the
in-distribution gaps further. The OOD probes are dataset-level shifts
between tasks of the same modality, evaluated with confidence-based
scores; no corruptions, adversarial perturbations or likelihood-based
methods were considered. Finally, the mechanism analysis is associational
by design; a causal decomposition of the ensemble gain into
member-quality and diversity components would require interventions on
each ingredient separately.

% ─────────────────────────────── 7 conclusion ───────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared four ways of spending a fixed training budget on the
uncertainty of one classifier, holding compute, data, optimizer and (in
part) checkpoints identical across conditions. Budget-split deep ensembles
gave the best-calibrated in-distribution uncertainty in every setting
tested, an advantage that grew with the budget and survived per-condition
temperature scaling; single-trajectory strategies captured part of the
benefit on tasks with calibration headroom and none of it on a saturated
task; and only prediction averaging improved out-of-distribution
detection, on every probe and direction tested, while weight averaging of
the same checkpoints showed no reliable improvement. The paired,
seed-replicated, checkpoint-matched template used here is directly reusable
for larger networks, harder shifts and richer recalibration families, and
we release all code and raw records to make such extensions cheap. In the
regime we measured, the practical answer to ``how should a fixed training
budget be spent for calibrated uncertainty?'' is: across independent
restarts, not along one trajectory.

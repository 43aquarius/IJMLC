averaging for calibrated uncertainty under fixed training
budgets\thanks{Preprint prepared for submission
to the International Journal of Machine Learning and Cybernetics.}}
\author{Chang Tan\thanks{Corresponding author: aquars43@foxmail.com}\,%
\thanks{Dalian University of Technology, Dalian, China.}}
\date{13 October 2026}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Practitioners needing calibrated predictive uncertainty face a fixed
training budget, yet method comparisons typically train each method to
convergence; we ask how to spend it. Under an identical 60-epoch SGD budget we compared four allocation
strategies for a multilayer perceptron on Fashion-MNIST: one long-trained network
(\single); three independently restarted 20-epoch networks averaged by
prediction (\deepens); three cycle-end snapshots of one cyclic run (\snapshot); and their uniform weight average
(\swa). Across ten replications, every strategy improved test
negative log-likelihood (NLL) over \single{} (Holm $P=0.0098$),
with the ordering \deepens{} $>$ \snapshot{} $>$ \swa{} $>$ \single{} on
NLL and calibration error. The ordering was robust across budgets
(30--120 epochs), ensemble sizes ($k=3$, $5$), a reversed dataset
direction (MNIST in-distribution, where only \deepens{} separated from
\single{}) and, post-hoc, a small convolutional network on CIFAR-10, with bounded exceptions at the lowest budget. In
out-of-distribution (OOD) detection, prediction averaging improved over
\single{} on every probe and direction with the largest gains; weight averaging of the identical checkpoints showed smaller,
task-dependent effects ($-0.016$ to $+0.025$), significant on some
settings, reversed on one. Per-condition post-hoc temperature scaling
repaired much of every condition's miscalibration yet left the
budget-split ensemble significantly best at the core budget and above
(NLL 0.289 vs 0.333 calibrated \single{}), while largely
equalising the ensembles at the lowest budget. Evidence is bounded to small networks and small-scale image tasks, but there it
supports a simple rule where calibration headroom exists:
split the budget across independent restarts rather than extending
training or averaging weights.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Modern classifiers achieve high accuracy yet assign miscalibrated
confidence: networks trained for many epochs with small mini-batches become
systematically overconfident, so their predicted probabilities cannot be
interpreted at face value
\citep{guo2017,abdar2021}. Because downstream decisions, selective
prediction and abstention all consume these probabilities, miscalibration
is a deployment risk rather than a cosmetic defect, and a large body of work
now addresses uncertainty quantification in deep learning
\citep{abdar2021,ovadia2019}. Among the available remedies, three families
dominate empirical practice. Deep ensembles average the predictions of
independently trained networks and set a strong but compute-expensive
baseline \citep{lakshminarayanan2017,fort2019}. Single-trajectory
approximations reuse one training run: snapshot ensembles collect the
checkpoints left behind by cyclic learning-rate schedules
\citep{huang2017,loshchilov2017}, and stochastic weight averaging (\swa)
averages network weights along a (typically cyclic) trajectory
\citep{izmailov2018,wilson2020,maddox2019}. Variational and Bayesian
approximations such as Monte Carlo dropout form a third family
\citep{gal2016,welling2011}.

These families are usually compared at their own best operating points:
each method is tuned until it stops improving, and the reported trade-off is
accuracy or calibration against the \emph{added} cost of the method
\citep{ashukha2020}. Practitioners, however, face the inverse problem: a
fixed compute allocation and a choice of how to spend it. Under a budget of
$B$ epochs one may train one network for $B$ epochs, $k$ networks for
$B/k$ epochs each, one cyclic run of $B$ epochs whose cycle-end snapshots
form an ensemble, or the weight average of those snapshots. Benchmark
studies have mapped the performance of uncertainty methods under
distribution shift and documented the pitfalls of in-domain evaluation
\citep{ovadia2019,ashukha2020}, but they vary budgets across methods. The
question of which \emph{allocation} of a fixed budget buys the
best-calibrated uncertainty is therefore only partially answered, and the
mechanism behind the expected ordering---member quality versus member
diversity, prediction averaging versus weight averaging---is rarely
isolated within one controlled experiment.

We addressed this gap with a four-way, budget-matched, seed-paired
comparison in which every strategy consumed exactly $B$ epochs of the same
optimizer, architecture, data split and learning-rate ceiling. Two design
choices make the contrasts unusually clean. First, the first ensemble
member of the budget-split ensemble and the first cyclic snapshot are the
same network (verified bit-identical), so the \deepens{}--\snapshot{}
contrast isolates the effect of restarting training independently versus
continuing one trajectory. Second, \snapshot{} and \swa{} combine the
\emph{identical} checkpoints, so their contrast isolates the combination
mechanism---prediction space versus parameter space---with no trajectory
noise. The pre-registered core setting (Fashion-MNIST, $B=60$, $k=3$, ten
seeds) is extended along five axes: total budget ($B \in
\{30,60,120\}$), ensemble size ($k \in \{3,5\}$), dataset direction
(MNIST in-distribution with Fashion-MNIST as the shift probe, in addition
to the reverse), OOD probe identity (MNIST and KMNIST), a post-hoc
temperature-scaling baseline fitted per condition on validation data
\citep{guo2017,zhang2020}, and---as a post-hoc architecture extension---a
small convolutional network trained on CIFAR-10 with CIFAR-100 as a
natural-image shift probe.

The contributions of this paper are ascriptive rather than algorithmic; we
offer a controlled measurement and a mechanism account, not a new method.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A compute-exact four-way comparison design} in which the
budget-split ensemble, the snapshot ensemble and the weight average share
training compute, data and (in part) checkpoints, with seed-paired exact
permutation inference throughout (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item \textbf{A robust ordering result on the primary outcome.} Splitting
a fixed budget across independent restarts produced the best-calibrated
uncertainty on NLL and ECE in every setting tested, and this ordering was
stable across budgets, ensemble sizes, both dataset directions and a
CIFAR-10 replication with a convolutional architecture
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-main}, \ref{sec:results-robust},
\ref{sec:results-cifar}).
\item \textbf{A boundary condition.} On the near-saturated MNIST task the
single-trajectory strategies stopped separating from the single long-trained
network, whereas the budget-split ensemble remained significantly better;
the ensemble effect in this regime co-occurs with---and plausibly
requires---headroom for longer training to damage calibration
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-robust}).
\item \textbf{A separation of combination mechanisms under shift.} Averaging
predictions improved OOD detection on every probe--direction combination,
with the largest and most consistent gains; averaging the same weights
showed small, task-dependent effects---significant on some probes and
budgets, absent or reversed on others
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ood}).
\item \textbf{A post-hoc recalibration stress test.} Temperature scaling
fitted per condition compressed in-distribution differences but did not
close them at the core budget or above; it shifted OOD detection only
slightly on the digit tasks (at most $+0.012$ AUROC) but more visibly on
CIFAR-10 (up to $+0.026$), and at the lowest budget it largely equalised
the ensemble strategies (Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ts}).
\item \textbf{A cross-architecture replication.} The full ordering, the
OOD separation and the member-level mechanism measurements replicate on a
small convolutional network trained on CIFAR-10 with a natural-image shift
probe (Sect.~\ref{sec:results-cifar}).
\end{itemize}

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the study
against prior work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} specifies the design,
metrics and statistical analysis. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
results organized by research question, Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
interpretation, limitations and practical guidance, and
Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Deep ensembles and their mechanism}
\citet{lakshminarayanan2017} showed that averaging the predictive
distributions of independently trained networks is a simple, scalable and
strongly competitive uncertainty estimator, and subsequent studies confirmed
its robustness under dataset shift \citep{ovadia2019}.
\citet{fort2019} analysed \emph{why} restarts help: independently
initialised networks converge to different basins of the loss landscape, so
their errors decorrelate. That analysis, however, treats ensembles as an
\emph{added} cost; the present study instead asks what restarts buy when
the budget is fixed and each member is correspondingly shorter.
\citet{ashukha2020} is closest in spirit: their ``pitfalls'' study of
in-domain uncertainty includes compute-matched single-versus-ensemble
comparisons and finds ensembles remain preferable. Our design differs in
two respects: we include the two single-trajectory strategies (snapshot
ensembles and \swa) that a budget-constrained practitioner would actually
choose between, and we hold checkpoints identical between the
prediction-averaging and weight-averaging conditions so that the combination
mechanism is isolated exactly. Related fixed-resource questions have been
asked for \emph{memory}: \citet{chirkova2020} compare one wide network with
several thinner ones at a fixed parameter count, and applied studies adopt
cheaper ensemble approximations for industrial reliability
\citep{thuy2024}; neither addresses training-budget allocation or the
calibration and OOD endpoints measured here.

\paragraph{Single-trajectory ensembles}
Snapshot ensembles exploit warm restarts \citep{loshchilov2017}: one
cyclic-learning-rate run yields several ``free'' models at the cycle minima
\citep{huang2017}. \swa{} instead averages the weights of checkpoints along
a trajectory and lands in flatter, wider regions associated with better
generalisation \citep{izmailov2018}; Bayesian readings and Gaussian
generalisations of the same idea include the work of
\citet{wilson2020} and \citet{maddox2019}. A recent large-scale benchmark
of checkpoint averaging on AlgoPerf finds that it accelerates training and
mildly improves generalisation \citep{ajroldi2025}; that evidence concerns
optimisation speed and accuracy rather than uncertainty quality, and does
not include the checkpoint-matched contrast with prediction averaging that
isolates the combination mechanism here. Related approximations reduce
ensemble cost by sharing parameters, e.g.\ BatchEnsemble
\citep{wen2020}. These works establish what each strategy achieves at its
own budget; the budget-matched trade-off among them, and in particular the
contrast between combining the same checkpoints in function space versus
parameter space, is what we measure. We also evaluate a denser \swa{}
variant that averages every epoch-end checkpoint, following standard \swa{}
practice \citep{izmailov2018}, as an exploratory bridge.

\paragraph{Calibration and post-hoc recalibration}
Overconfidence of modern networks and the expected calibration error were
popularised as practical metrics by \citet{guo2017}, who also introduced the
temperature-scaling (\ts) post-hoc fix we adopt: a single temperature fitted
on held-out data to minimise validation NLL. \citet{naeini2015} provide the
binning foundation for calibration measurement. Compositional post-hoc
calibration \citep{zhang2020} shows that calibration and ensembling
interact; the question we isolate is narrower and practical: does per-condition
\ts{} remove the advantage that budget-split restarts hold over longer
training, or does it merely compress the differences? Our setting extends
the classic observation that ensembles remain better calibrated than
recalibrated single models \citep{lakshminarayanan2017} to the
budget-matched regime.

\paragraph{Uncertainty under distribution shift}
The maximum-softmax score of \citet{hendrycks2017} is the canonical
baseline for detecting out-of-distribution inputs, and confidence-based
scores are known to be brittle \citep{nguyen2015}. \citet{ovadia2019}
benchmarked a broad set of uncertainty methods under shift, finding deep
ensembles the most robust; that study, however, varies training budgets
across methods and does not include the checkpoint-matched
snapshot-versus-\swa{} contrast that separates prediction averaging from
weight averaging here. Our OOD analysis uses two probes in each dataset
direction (MNIST and KMNIST against Fashion-MNIST in-distribution, and the
reverse), which lets us check that conclusions are not specific to one
probe.

\paragraph{Positioning}
No prior study that we are aware of isolates, within a single controlled
experiment, (i) the allocation of a fixed budget across restarts versus one
long run, (ii) the combination of identical checkpoints by prediction
averaging versus weight averaging, and (iii) the interaction of both with
post-hoc recalibration and OOD detection; a re-check of the 2024--2025
literature (including recent weight-averaging benchmarks,
\citealp{ajroldi2025}) did not surface a study covering any of these three
contrasts. We claim novelty for the \emph{comparison design and
measurement}, not for any of the component methods, all of which are
standard.

\section{Study design and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Budget-matched conditions}\label{sec:conditions}

The total training budget $B$ is fixed, and four allocation strategies
consume it in different ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}a). \single{}
trains one network for $B$ epochs under a single cosine anneal from learning
rate 0.1 to 0. \deepens (budget-split deep ensemble) trains $k$
independently initialised networks for $B/k$ epochs each, each under its own
complete cosine anneal, and averages their predictive distributions.
\snapshot trains one network for $B$ epochs under $k$ cosine
cycles \citep{loshchilov2017} and keeps the $k$ cycle-end checkpoints
\citep{huang2017}. \swa takes the uniform weight average of those
same $k$ checkpoints \citep{izmailov2018}. Throughout, the pre-registered
core setting is Fashion-MNIST with $B=60$ and $k=3$; the extension settings
vary $B \in \{30,120\}$, $k=5$, and the in-distribution dataset
(Sect.~\ref{sec:datasets}).

Two design details strengthen the comparisons. First, the first cosine
cycle of the cyclic run is, by construction, a complete $B/k$-epoch anneal
with a specified initialisation and data order; we verified
programmatically that this checkpoint is bit-identical to an independent
$B/k$-epoch run under the same seed, and used it as both the first
\snapshot{} member and the first \deepens{} member. The implementation is
therefore compute-exact ($2B + (k{-}1)B/k$ epochs per replicate) while the two
ensemble conditions differ, from the second member onward, precisely in
whether subsequent members restart independently or continue the
trajectory. Second, \snapshot{} and \swa{} are derived from the same run, so
their contrast measures only how the identical checkpoints are combined: by
averaging predictions (function space) or weights (parameter space). We
note that our \swa{} averages exactly $k$ cycle-end checkpoints in order to
remain checkpoint-matched with \snapshot{}; this is a restricted \swa{}
schedule relative to the many-checkpoint averages of standard practice
\citep{izmailov2018}, and an exploratory dense variant averaging every
epoch-end checkpoint is reported in the supplementary material.

Four cost dimensions must be kept distinct when interpreting these
comparisons. (i) \emph{Training budget} is exactly matched: every condition
consumes the same number of optimizer updates over the same data (identical
batch size and schedule family), which is the axis held fixed throughout.
(ii) \emph{Wall-clock time} is matched only to the extent that one epoch of
each condition has the same cost; total run time differs across studies
(model family) but never across conditions within a study. (iii)
\emph{Inference cost} is \emph{not} matched: both prediction-averaging
strategies (\deepens{} and \snapshot{}) require $k$ forward passes per
prediction, whereas \single{} and \swa{} require one. (iv) \emph{Total
deployment cost} therefore depends on the ratio of training to inference
workloads: at high query volumes the $k$-fold inference overhead can offset
the calibration advantage of prediction averaging, and in such regimes the
uncalibrated-but-cheap single-trajectory conditions may be preferred. All
accuracy, calibration and OOD comparisons below are performance statements
at matched training budget, not total-cost statements.

\subsection{Datasets and splits}\label{sec:datasets}

Two dataset directions are used. In the primary direction, the
in-distribution task is Fashion-MNIST \citep{xiao2017} (60{,}000 training
and 10{,}000 test images, $28\times28$ grayscale, ten classes) and the OOD
probes are the MNIST \citep{lecun1998} and KMNIST \citep{clanuwat2018}
test sets (10{,}000 images each). In the reversed direction, MNIST is the
in-distribution task and the Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST test sets serve as
probes. KMNIST (handwritten cursive Japanese characters) provides a
second, visually closer shift probe whose label spaces do not overlap with
either in-distribution task. In each direction, the 60{,}000-image training
set was split once, with a fixed documented split seed, into 54{,}000
training and 6{,}000 validation images. Images were scaled to $[0,1]$ and
standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the corresponding
training split; OOD probes were standardised with the \emph{in-distribution}
statistics, matching the deployment situation in which a shift is not known
in advance. The validation split was used only for monitoring, learning
curves and temperature fitting (Sect.~\ref{sec:metrics}); no early
stopping, hyperparameter selection or model selection was performed on
validation or test data. The test set and the OOD probes were evaluated
exactly once per condition per replicate, after all training was complete.

For the post-hoc cross-architecture extension, the in-distribution task is
CIFAR-10 \citep{krizhevsky2009} (50{,}000 training images, $32\times32$
colour, ten classes; 45{,}000/5{,}000 train--validation split under a new
documented split seed) with the CIFAR-100 test set (10{,}000 images, 100
fine-grained classes) as the OOD probe, standardised with the CIFAR-10
train-split statistics under the same unknown-shift policy. No data
augmentation was used in any setting, mirroring the core protocol and
keeping budget accounting exact.

\subsection{Architecture and training}\label{sec:training}

All Fashion-MNIST and MNIST networks were multilayer perceptrons with layers
784--128--64--10 and ReLU activations ($\approx$110k parameters), without
dropout or batch normalisation. The CIFAR-10 extension uses a small
convolutional network: three convolutional blocks (16, 32 and 64
$3\times3$ filters, each followed by ReLU and $2\times2$ max-pooling) and a
single fully connected head ($\approx$34k parameters), likewise without batch
normalisation or dropout. Batch normalisation is deliberately avoided in
both architectures: its running statistics interact with weight averaging
and would confound the checkpoint-matched \snapshot{}--\swa{} contrast, and
the study holds normalisation absent across settings for internal
consistency. Training used mini-batch SGD with batch size 256 (211 steps
per epoch on 54{,}000 images; 176 on 45{,}000), momentum 0.9 and weight
decay $10^{-4}$ (coupled). Learning rates followed per-step cosine schedules
from $\eta_{\max}=0.1$ to $0$: a single $B$-epoch anneal for \single{}, $k$
cycles for the cyclic run, and one $B/k$-epoch anneal per \deepens{}
member. Initialisation used PyTorch's default Kaiming-uniform scheme; the
data order of each run was generated from a dedicated seeded generator, with
the same seed convention in the CIFAR extension. Hyperparameters were
fixed from common practice before any test-set evaluation, and a pilot
confirmed training stability using training and validation losses only. On
two CPU cores, a $B=60$ MLP replicate took 72--87\,s of wall-clock time; the
extension settings required about 9{,}000 training epochs (69 minutes of
wall-clock time); and the CIFAR replication roughly 1{,}600 further epochs
(about 3.5 hours). Together with the pre-registered core and the
supplementary dense-\swa{} pass the complete study comprises roughly 12{,}800
epochs of single-machine CPU time.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

All metrics were computed on the full test set (and, for OOD, the full
probe) with probabilities evaluated in float64. NLL is the mean negative
log-probability of the true class under the (ensemble-mean) predictive
distribution. Accuracy is the fraction of argmax-correct predictions. The
Brier score is the mean over samples of the summed squared error between
the predictive distribution and the one-hot label. Expected calibration
error (ECE) uses 15 equal-width bins over maximum predicted probability
\citep{naeini2015,guo2017}, with 10 bins as a sensitivity check. OOD
detection uses the AUROC of the maximum-softmax score
\citep{hendrycks2017} with in-distribution samples as the positive class,
and the negative-entropy score as a sensitivity check. Ensemble disagreement
is the mean over test samples and member pairs of the Jensen--Shannon
divergence between member predictive distributions (natural logarithm).
Ensemble predictive distributions are the arithmetic mean of member softmax
outputs.

\paragraph{Temperature scaling}
Following \citet{guo2017}, we fit a single temperature $T$ per condition
and replicate by minimising NLL on the 6{,}000 validation images, and apply
it to the test and probe sets. The scaled predictive distribution is
$\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)$ where $z$ is the effective logit vector of the
condition: the raw logits for single-model conditions, and the logarithm of
the averaged probabilities for ensemble conditions (an averaged ensemble
has no raw logits of its own). For single networks this coincides exactly
with standard temperature scaling, because the softmax is shift-invariant
and $\log p$ differs from the raw logits only by a constant per sample.
The fitted temperature itself is reported as an overconfidence diagnostic.
This per-condition \ts{} is the strongest simple post-hoc recalibration
baseline \citep{guo2017,zhang2020}: it asks whether the between-strategy
differences survive when every strategy is allowed its own best
recalibration.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}

The analysis of the core setting (including the contrast set, the primary
outcome and the multiplicity strategy) was written and stored before any
test-set evaluation; the only amendment (increasing replications from five
to ten) was made after a timing pilot, before any outcome was observed.
The independent experimental unit is the seed; each seed contributes one
value per condition per metric, and all contrasts are within-seed paired
differences. We report mean differences with two-sided 95\% $t$-intervals
($n-1=9$ degrees of freedom), exact two-sided sign-flip permutation $P$
values enumerating all $2^{10}$ sign patterns, and Holm--Bonferroni
adjustment across the five pre-specified contrasts applied to the primary
outcome (NLL). With ten seeds the uncorrected two-sided sign-flip floor is
$2/2^{10}=0.0020$ (Holm floor 0.0098), so every contrast reported at that
value is one in which all ten seeds agree in direction---we note this
wherever the floor value appears. The five contrasts are \deepens$-$\single,
\snapshot$-$\single, \swa$-$\single, \deepens$-$\snapshot{} and
\snapshot$-$\swa{}; the first three measure what each strategy buys over
spending the whole budget on one network, and the last two locate the
ordering. The extension settings ($B$, $k$, dataset direction, probes), the
temperature-scaled analyses, and the CIFAR-10 cross-architecture
replication were not part of the pre-registered plan and are labelled as
such; each is analysed with the same machinery and its own Holm family over
the same five contrasts on NLL, and the \ts{} family on \ts-NLL is likewise
Holm-corrected. OOD contrasts are secondary: their primary reporting is the
uncorrected exact permutation $P$ (five contrasts per probe), as
pre-specified. In addition, a post-hoc robustness analysis---defined after
the CIFAR results were known, and labelled as such wherever it appears---
applies Holm correction within each setting--probe--score family of five
contrasts, mirroring the primary family structure, with a setting-level and
a global sensitivity (Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ood}; Supplementary
Table~S4). No OOD conclusion below rests on the choice among these
families: all of them are reported for every strategy. For the CIFAR replication the hypotheses and analysis plan
were fixed in writing before any test-set evaluation took place, but the
setting itself is post-hoc. Sensitivity analyses (10-bin ECE, entropy-based
AUROC score, dense \swa{}) were specified in advance or, for dense \swa{},
labelled exploratory. No data were excluded; no condition, replicate or
sample was dropped at any stage.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

Experiments used PyTorch 2.14.0 (CPU), NumPy 2.1.3, SciPy 1.14.1 and
scikit-learn 1.5.2 on Python 3.12 (two CPU cores, no GPU). Every training
run is deterministic given its replicate seed; the v2 pipeline used for the
extension settings reproduces the pre-registered v1 records exactly (a
seed-0 re-run matched all 28 recorded metrics bit-for-bit, and the shared
first member passed bit-identity checks in both pipelines), and a
clean-environment gate (fresh virtual environment, fresh data download,
refactored path-independent code in a fresh directory) reproduced all 110
recorded seed-0 metric values, including per-bin reliability entries,
bit-for-bit. The CIFAR pipeline additionally re-verified the shared-first-
member bit-identity and the exact equivalence of its sharded, checkpointed
execution against one-shot training. Training, analysis and figure
scripts, the raw per-replicate metric records (JSONL), the aggregated
analysis outputs and the per-panel figure source data are released in a
public repository
(\url{https://github.com/43aquarius/IJMLC}); datasets are publicly
available \citep{xiao2017,lecun1998,clanuwat2018,krizhevsky2009}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Budget-matched comparison in the core setting}
\label{sec:results-main}

All three ensembling strategies improved the primary outcome, test NLL,
over \single{} at matched budget (Table~\ref{tab:contrasts},
Fig.~\ref{fig:main}b): mean paired differences were $-0.2245$ (95\% CI
$[-0.2360,-0.2130]$) for \deepens{}, $-0.1780$ for \snapshot{} and
$-0.1396$ for \swa{}, each with exact permutation $P=0.0020$ and
Holm-adjusted $P=0.0098$ across the five pre-specified contrasts. The
strategies were also strictly ordered: \deepens{} improved on \snapshot{}
by $-0.0465$ ($[-0.0505,-0.0425]$) and \snapshot{} improved on \swa{} by
$-0.0384$ ($[-0.0407,-0.0360]$), both with adjusted $P=0.0098$. The
single long-trained network combined the highest per-epoch accuracy
trajectory with the worst predictive log-likelihood: its NLL of $0.5265
\pm 0.0159$ (mean $\pm$ s.d.) coexisted with accuracy of $0.8895 \pm
0.0015$, reflecting severe overconfidence rather than classification
error.

The same ordering held on every secondary in-distribution metric in the
core setting
(Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). Accuracy improved modestly but consistently,
the Brier score and ECE followed the NLL ordering, and the effect sizes
were stable across replicates (all ten replicates favoured every strategy
over \single{} for NLL). The reliability diagrams locate the differences
in the confidence scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}c, Supplementary Fig.~S1): the
single network's predictions clustered at high confidence with accuracy
below that confidence (ECE $0.0702 \pm 0.0021$; a typical predicted
confidence of about $0.96$ was matched by roughly $0.89$ empirical
accuracy), \deepens{} nearly restored calibration (ECE $0.0236 \pm
0.0008$, a 66\% reduction), \snapshot{} removed about a third of the error
and \swa{} about a quarter. These calibration gains, rather than accuracy,
carried most of the NLL improvement.

\subsection{Robustness across budgets, ensemble sizes and dataset direction}
\label{sec:results-robust}

The extension settings test whether the core ordering is an artefact of
one budget, one ensemble size or one dataset direction
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}, Table~\ref{tab:extension}). Across budgets
$B \in \{30, 60, 120\}$ the NLL ordering \deepens{} $>$ \snapshot{} $>$
\swa{} $>$ \single{} was preserved, with all five contrasts
Holm-adjusted $P=0.0098$ at every budget; the ECE ordering was likewise
preserved at every budget. The budget-split advantage \emph{grew} with the
budget: the \deepens$-$\single{} difference widened
from $-0.0754$ at $B=30$ to $-0.2245$ at $B=60$ and $-0.3317$ at $B=120$,
because longer training made the single network progressively more
overconfident (its NLL rose from $0.3795$ to $0.5265$ to $0.6672$ while
its accuracy was flat) while the 20- or 40-epoch restart members stayed
well calibrated. Enlarging the ensemble from $k=3$ to $k=5$ at fixed
budget improved every ensemble strategy (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}b; \single{}
is unchanged by construction) and preserved the NLL ordering
(\deepens{} $-0.2290$ vs.\ \single{}, adjusted $P=0.0098$).

The secondary metrics were \emph{not} uniformly ordered in every setting,
and the exceptions are informative. In the core setting and at $B=120$ the
strict four-way ordering held on every in-distribution metric
(Table~\ref{tab:conditions}), but at $B=30$ accuracy was highest for
\snapshot{} (0.8929) and lowest for \deepens{} (0.8905, versus 0.8917 for
\single{}; paired \deepens$-$\single{} $-0.0012$ $[-0.0021,-0.0002]$,
uncorrected $P=0.018$), and \snapshot{} also attained the best Brier
score (0.1562 versus 0.1573 for \deepens{}). At $k=5$, \snapshot{}'s
accuracy was nominally highest (0.8944 versus 0.8938). All such reversals
are bounded by $0.003$ in magnitude, appear only at the lowest budget or
the larger ensemble size, and never affect NLL or ECE; we therefore claim
the strict every-metric ordering only for the core setting and $B=120$,
and the NLL/ECE ordering across all Fashion-MNIST settings. They
qualitatively match the budget-sweep account: at $B=30$ the single network
is not yet overconfident (ECE 0.049 versus 0.070 at $B=60$), so the
between-strategy gaps that calibration carries are small.

Reversing the dataset direction exposed a boundary condition. With MNIST
as the in-distribution task (accuracy $0.982$ for \single{}; ECE $0.0098$),
only \deepens{} separated from \single{} on NLL ($-0.0158$, 95\% CI
$[-0.0183,-0.0134]$, adjusted $P=0.0098$); the single-trajectory
strategies showed no reliable improvement (\snapshot{} $-0.0015$,
$P=0.41$; \swa{} $+0.0017$, $P=0.41$; Fig.~\ref{fig:direction}a). The
ensemble effect documented here---longer training damages calibration and
averaging repairs it---is observed only where such damage exists; the
near-saturated MNIST task provides no such headroom, its single 60-epoch
network already being well calibrated (ECE $0.0098$ versus $0.0702$ on
Fashion-MNIST). The budget-split restarts nevertheless remained the best
allocation on NLL even on MNIST: \deepens{} still beat \snapshot{}
($-0.0143$, adjusted $P=0.0098$), and by adjacency also \swa{} (the
\snapshot$-$\swa{} contrast was itself significant at adjusted
$P=0.0098$).

\subsection{Post-hoc temperature scaling compresses but does not close the
gaps}\label{sec:results-ts}

A natural objection is that the between-strategy differences in NLL reflect
fixable overconfidence rather than information content: a single temperature
fitted on validation data repairs much of a network's miscalibration at
negligible cost \citep{guo2017}. We therefore fitted one temperature per
condition and replicate (Fig.~\ref{fig:ts}) and re-evaluated all metrics.
Temperature scaling behaved as expected: it helped every condition, and
most dramatically the single network, whose NLL fell from $0.5265$ to
$0.3325$ and whose ECE fell from $0.0702$ to $0.0111$. The fitted
temperatures mirror the overconfidence ordering (\single{} $2.46$,
\swa{} $1.79$, \snapshot{} $1.58$, \deepens{} $1.25$): the strategies that
looked worst calibrated uncalibrated needed the most cooling.

Two findings survive the recalibration at the core budget and above,
with one boundary case at the lowest budget. First, the ordering
persisted: after per-condition \ts{}, \deepens{} still attained the lowest
NLL ($0.2891 \pm 0.0020$) and every contrast in the five-contrast family
remained significant at Holm-adjusted $P=0.0098$ (\deepens$-$\single{}
$-0.0434$, 95\% CI $[-0.0468,-0.0400]$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ts}a). The same
held at $B=120$, at $k=5$ and in the MNIST direction (all \deepens{}
versus \single{} adjusted $P=0.0098$; Supplementary Table~S3). At
$B=30$, however, recalibration largely equalised the ensemble
strategies: \ts-adjusted NLLs spanned only 0.2986--0.3126, and \snapshot{}
was nominally best, with the \deepens$-$\snapshot{} difference on
\ts-NLL reversed ($+0.0026$ $[+0.0002,+0.0050]$, adjusted $P=0.045$).
Budget-split restarts therefore do not merely produce a conveniently
recalibratable distribution---at and beyond the core budget, averaging
independent restarts yields information that a monotone rescaling of any
single trajectory cannot recover---but at small budgets, where the single
network is only mildly overconfident, post-hoc calibration is a genuine
partial substitute. Second, on the digit tasks recalibration left the
OOD results where they were: \ts{} changed OOD AUROC by at most $+0.012$
on any condition or probe (e.g.\ \single{} $0.7533 \rightarrow 0.7620$ on
the MNIST probe, \deepens{} $0.8502 \rightarrow 0.8566$), leaving the
between-strategy differences intact. On CIFAR-10 the shifts were larger
(\single{} $+0.026$, \swa{} $+0.019$, \snapshot{} $+0.017$, \deepens{}
$+0.004$), compressing the \deepens$-$\single{} gap from $+0.069$ to
$+0.046$ without reversing any ordering
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ood}, \ref{sec:results-cifar}). This heterogeneity
is expected in principle: temperature scaling transforms each sample's
effective logits monotonically, but the maximum-softmax score it induces
need not preserve the cross-sample ranking of the unscaled score, so its
effect on confidence-based OOD separation is an empirical property of the
condition and the shift rather than a guaranteed null.

\subsection{OOD detection separates prediction averaging from weight
averaging}\label{sec:results-ood}

We next asked which budget allocations buy robustness, measured as the
separation between in-distribution test images and each OOD probe using
the maximum-softmax score (Fig.~\ref{fig:direction}b). In the core
setting, \deepens{} improved AUROC by $+0.0969$ over \single{} on the MNIST
probe (95\% CI $[+0.0847,+0.1091]$, $P=0.0020$) and by $+0.1097$
($[+0.1004,+0.1190]$) on the KMNIST probe, whereas \swa{}'s effects were
an order of magnitude smaller and inconsistent across probes: $-0.0029$
($[-0.0166,+0.0109]$) on the MNIST probe ($P=0.64$) and $+0.0112$
($[+0.0023,+0.0202]$) on KMNIST ($P=0.023$; OOD contrasts are secondary and
their primary reporting is uncorrected---five contrasts per probe, so
uncorrected $P$ values near 0.05 should be read cautiously). \snapshot{} occupied an intermediate
position ($+0.0423$ and $+0.0585$ respectively). Because \snapshot{} and
\swa{} combine the identical three checkpoints, this difference is
attributable to the combination mechanism alone: averaging predictions
transfers the members' ability to disagree on shifted inputs into the
score, while averaging weights collapses the members into one network
that recovers only part of that transferred signal, and how large that
part is varies with the task and the shift
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-cifar}). We note that the width of the \swa{} interval
(about $0.028$ AUROC units) bounds what this design can resolve; the claim
is one of absence of evidence at this precision, not of evidence of
absence.

The pattern generalised across probes and directions, and the extension
settings let us state it over all eleven combinations of setting and probe
(Supplementary Table~S4). Prediction averaging improved detection
everywhere: \deepens{} attained the best mean detection on every
combination, and its improvement over \single{} was the only one
significant on all eleven within each probe's five-contrast family
(Holm-adjusted $P \leq 0.031$; in the MNIST direction: $+0.0139$,
$P=0.008$, and $+0.0235$, $P=0.002$, uncorrected as above).
\snapshot{} was significant on nine of eleven combinations, failing only
in the MNIST direction. Weight averaging, in contrast, was small and
inconsistent: its \swa$-$\single{} effects ranged from $-0.016$ to
$+0.025$, significantly positive on five combinations (including the core
setting's KMNIST probe, $+0.0112$, adjusted $P=0.023$, and both probes at
$B=30$), significantly negative on one ($-0.016$ on the MNIST probe at
$B=120$, adjusted $P=0.012$), and null on the remaining five; on CIFAR-10
it retained roughly a third of \deepens{}'s gain
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-cifar}). Under the most conservative global
family---all 55 MSP contrasts across settings and probes---no OOD contrast
survives, \deepens{} included, because the per-test resolution floor
($0.0020$) times the family size exceeds $0.05$; the OOD conclusions
therefore rest on effect size and cross-setting consistency rather than
on any single global threshold. The same pattern held with the
negative-entropy score (\swa{} effects $-0.015$ to $+0.031$), so the
conclusion is not specific to the score function, and the exploratory
dense \swa{} variant (40-checkpoint average) likewise left OOD detection
unchanged from \single{} (Supplementary Table~S2).

\subsection{Mechanism: member calibration and member diversity}
\label{sec:results-mech}

The design also pins down why \deepens{} dominates \snapshot{} at matched
budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}e,f); because the two ensembles share their
first member, this contrast is carried by the later members, where the
strategies differ in restarting versus continuing training. First, the
individual members differed in calibration: the three independent 20-epoch
restarts achieved a mean member NLL of $0.3379 \pm 0.0022$, whereas the
second and third cyclic snapshots (taken after 40 and 60 cumulative epochs
of continued training) were individually more overconfident ($0.4039 \pm
0.0087$ averaged over members), even though their member accuracy was
nearly identical ($0.8912$ versus $0.8916$). Continued training on the
same trajectory thus bought no member-level accuracy but cost
member-level calibration. Second, the members were less diverse: the mean
pairwise Jensen--Shannon divergence of member predictive distributions was
$0.0150 \pm 0.0003$ nats for \deepens{} but only $0.0106 \pm 0.0003$ nats
for \snapshot{}, a 30\% reduction in disagreement. At $k=5$ the same two
ingredients pointed the same way (member NLL $0.3212$ versus $0.3796$; JSD
$0.0101$ versus $0.0107$, the diversity gap narrowing as the shorter
12-epoch members become more similar). Both quantities point in the
direction that favours independent restarts; we note, however, that with
$n=10$ replicates and $k \leq 5$ members these measurements associate the
mechanism with the outcome rather than establishing it causally. The same
two ingredients replicate in the CIFAR-10 extension with much larger gaps
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results-cifar}).

\subsection{Cross-architecture replication: CIFAR-10 with a convolutional
network}\label{sec:results-cifar}

All results above were obtained with multilayer perceptrons on grayscale
digit tasks. To test whether the findings depend on that regime, we repeated
the full core design (B=60, $k=3$, ten seeds, identical optimiser protocol
and seed conventions) with a small convolutional network on CIFAR-10, using
the CIFAR-100 test set as a natural-image shift probe. This extension was
designed and its analysis plan fixed after the MLP results were known, and
is therefore labelled post-hoc throughout; the data were evaluated exactly
once, after all training was complete. The replication is summarised in
Fig.~\ref{fig:cifar} and Table~\ref{tab:cifar}.

The replication confirms every qualitative finding of the MLP study, with
larger margins. The single 60-epoch network was severely overconfident on
CIFAR-10 (NLL 1.9959 at accuracy 0.7086), and all three
strategies improved NLL over \single{} with the same ordering as on
Fashion-MNIST: \deepens{} 0.7070 $<$ \snapshot{} 0.8890 $<$ \swa{} 1.0740.
All five contrasts were significant after Holm adjustment within the
setting's family (all adjusted $P=0.0098$); the budget-split advantage
($-1.2889$ NLL, 95\% CI $[-1.3432,-1.2345]$) was several times larger than the
Fashion-MNIST core effect ($-0.2245$), and the pattern extended to every
in-distribution metric in means, including accuracy (\deepens{} $-$ \single{} $+0.0699$
$[+0.0627,+0.0772]$) and ECE (Table~\ref{tab:cifar}); on ECE, however, the
\deepens$-$\snapshot{} contrast was directionally consistent with the digit
tasks but not statistically resolved at $n=10$ ($-0.0418$,
$[-0.0845,+0.0008]$, uncorrected $P=0.082$; see the anomalous replicate
below), whereas \deepens$-$\single{} on ECE was significant ($-0.1805$,
$P=0.0020$). Under the
natural-image shift probe (CIFAR-100), the checkpoint-matched separation
replicated: prediction averaging improved OOD detection over \single{}
(\deepens{} $+0.0689$ $[+0.0635,+0.0743]$, \snapshot{} $+0.0393$), whereas
weight averaging of the identical checkpoints improved detection by less
($+0.0247$ $[+0.0224,+0.0269]$, uncorrected $P=0.002$, Holm-adjusted
$P=0.0098$ within the probe's family)---roughly two-thirds of \snapshot{}'s
improvement and one-third of \deepens{}'s. Weight averaging is therefore
not without OOD value in every regime; its effect here is genuine but
small relative to prediction averaging on the same checkpoints.
Per-condition temperature scaling again compressed but did not close the
gap (\deepens{} $-$ \single{} on \ts-NLL $-0.1827$, adjusted $P=0.0098$), and the
mechanism measurements pointed the same way as on Fashion-MNIST: restart
members were better calibrated individually than continued-training
snapshots (member NLL 1.0676 versus 1.2288) and more diverse
(mean pairwise JSD 0.1545 versus 0.0915 nats). The fitted
temperatures mirrored the overconfidence ordering
(\single{} 4.23, \swa{} 2.30, \snapshot{} 1.88, \deepens{} 1.14). As a replication, this
extension changes no claims' direction; it strengthens their external
validity by one architecture family and one natural-image dataset with a
semantically richer shift probe.

One of the ten replicates reversed the \deepens{}--\snapshot{} ordering at
seed level, and its cause is instructive. One of that replicate's two fresh
restarts collapsed within its first epoch to near-uniform predictions
(training loss at the $\ln 10$ level from epoch 1 onward, output logits
decaying to zero, accuracy near chance) and never recovered---an instance
of restart failure under the aggressive no-normalisation learning rate,
unique among the twenty fresh restarts of this extension. The event is
transparent in the released per-replicate records (member mean accuracy
0.515, ensemble NLL 0.929, ECE 0.210) and was verified by a deterministic
re-run of the three members with per-epoch logging; the shared first
member and the cyclic trajectory reproduce bit-identically, so the collapse
is a property of that restart's initialisation and schedule, not of the
data pipeline or the evaluation code. The replicate is retained in all
analyses, in line with the pre-specified no-exclusion rule; with it
included, every primary contrast remains significant, and a
leave-one-replicate-out sensitivity shows that dropping it strengthens
the \deepens$-$\snapshot{} contrasts (NLL $-0.2075$, ECE $-0.0607$, both
$P = 0.0039$) while leaving every other conclusion unchanged. The
ECE ordering over \snapshot{}---directionally consistent but unresolved
with all ten replicates---becomes significant without it; we report both
views and rest no claim on the exclusion. Restart failure of this kind is
itself a property of the budget-split strategy at this scale that
practitioners should weigh: a dead member degraded this replicate's
calibration substantially, yet the ensemble still beat the long-trained
\single{} network on NLL by a wide margin ($0.929$ versus $2.017$).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

At a fixed training budget, this study returns a consistent allocation
rule: splitting the budget across independent restarts (\deepens{})
produced the best-calibrated uncertainty on the primary outcome in every
setting we measured---both dataset directions, three budgets spanning a
$4\times$ range, two ensemble sizes, and a CIFAR-10 replication with a
convolutional architecture; a cyclic-schedule snapshot ensemble
(\snapshot{}) captured most of the in-distribution benefit at one run's
bookkeeping cost; and weight averaging (\swa{}) improved in-distribution
metrics but, unlike either prediction-averaging strategy, showed no
consistent OOD improvement: its effects ranged from $-0.016$ to $+0.025$
across the eleven setting--probe combinations, significantly positive on
some and significantly negative on one.
Because the four conditions shared data, architecture, optimizer, budget and
pairing by seed, and because \snapshot{} and \swa{} shared the identical
checkpoints, these differences are attributable to the allocation of the
budget and the mechanism of combination rather than to tuning or trajectory
noise. The one systematic exception---secondary-metric orderings at the
lowest budget and at $k=5$---never touched NLL or ECE and never exceeded
$0.003$ in magnitude.

The mechanism measurements suggest an interpretation in terms of where the
two averaging operations act. Prediction averaging operates in function
space: when members disagree, the mixture flattens the predictive
distribution, which is exactly the behaviour that repairs overconfidence
and that produces low-confidence predictions on shifted inputs. Weight
averaging operates in parameter space: it selects a flatter, lower-loss
point along the trajectory \citep{izmailov2018,wilson2020}, which improves
the network's fit--confidence trade-off in distribution, but it collapses
the members' disagreement into a single confident model and thereby
discards part of the epistemic signal that the shift detector consumes.
How large the discarded part is varies with the regime: on the digit tasks
it was essentially all of it, while on CIFAR-100 the weight average
retained about a third of the prediction-averaging gain, so the size of
the separation is itself task- and shift-dependent. Consistent
with this reading, member-level analysis showed that continued-training
snapshots are individually more overconfident than fresh restarts at equal
accuracy, and less diverse from one another; the snapshot ensemble
therefore starts from worse-calibrated, more correlated members than the
budget-split ensemble even at the same budget.

The budget sweep adds a time dimension to this account. Longer training of
the single network made it progressively more overconfident---its NLL
\emph{worsened} from $0.38$ at $B=30$ to $0.67$ at $B=120$ while accuracy
was flat---so the value of redirecting that budget into restarts grew
with the budget. The reversed dataset direction shows the complementary
boundary: on a near-saturated task where the long-trained single network
is already well calibrated, the single-trajectory strategies had nothing
left to repair and stopped separating from \single{}, while budget-split
restarts still helped. The ensemble effect documented here is therefore
not universal; it is a property of regimes with calibration headroom. The
MLP evidence suggests such headroom arises when training is long enough
to damage calibration without improving accuracy; we have not surveyed how
common this regime is among deployed systems, and the rule should be read
as conditional on verifying that the long-trained baseline is overconfident
(a single reliability diagram suffices).

Post-hoc recalibration is the strongest available objection to any
calibration comparison, and our answer is two-sided. In distribution,
per-condition temperature scaling is a genuine partial substitute,
especially at small budgets where the \ts-adjusted strategies clustered
within $0.014$ NLL of each other and the ensemble ordering itself was no
longer significant; at the core budget and above, however, the
calibrated budget-split ensemble remained significantly better than
every calibrated alternative. Under shift, temperature scaling left
detection essentially unchanged on the digit tasks (at most $+0.012$
AUROC) but shifted it more visibly on CIFAR-10 (up to $+0.026$, in
\single{}'s favour): it is not a rank-preserving transformation of
confidence scores in general, so its effect on a deployment that
consumes such scores should be checked directly rather than assumed null.
This refines the classical observation that ensembles remain
better calibrated than recalibrated single models
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017,guo2017,zhang2020}: in the budget-matched
regime, what post-hoc calibration cannot buy is precisely the
restart-induced diversity.

Our results are consistent with the literature that motivated the
comparison---deep ensembles are strong but assumed expensive
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017,fort2019}, snapshot ensembles are attractive
because one run yields several models \citep{huang2017}, and \swa{}
improves accuracy and in-distribution calibration
\citep{izmailov2018,wilson2020,ajroldi2025}---but they quantify the
trade-off under the constraint that matters in practice. The CIFAR-10
replication shows that the trade-off is not an artefact of multilayer
perceptrons or of grayscale digits: on a small convolutional network
trained on natural images, the same ordering appeared with much larger
margins (the single network's raw NLL was above 2.0), and
the checkpoint-matched separation between prediction averaging and weight
averaging carried over to a semantically richer natural-image shift probe.
The budget-matched framing reverses the usual
cost accounting: a deep ensemble of three 20-epoch runs
is not three times as expensive as one 60-epoch run; it \emph{is} the same
budget spent differently. Under that accounting, the ``expensive''
strategy was uniformly best in this regime, the ``free'' strategies
captured different fractions of its benefit, and the cheapest-to-deploy
single-trajectory variant (\swa{}) bought little and inconsistent shift
robustness ($-0.016$ to $+0.025$ across settings and probes). This
complements benchmark studies that vary budgets across methods
\citep{ovadia2019,ashukha2020} and echoes the observation that
independent initialisation is the ingredient most responsible for
ensemble gains \citep{fort2019}: here that ingredient survives a
three-fold reduction in per-member training.

For practitioners, the allocation rule we can support is as follows. At
small scale and fixed compute, prefer independent restarts over longer
training for calibrated uncertainty, provided the long-trained baseline is
in fact overconfident. If only a single trajectory is affordable,
prefer its snapshot ensemble over its weight average when shift robustness
matters. Apply temperature scaling for its in-distribution benefit: it is
nearly free and repaired overconfidence in every condition here, but its
effect on confidence-based shift scores was small and not guaranteed null
in general (up to $+0.026$ AUROC on CIFAR-10), so verify it in the target
shift setting. Budget for the inference side as well: prediction averaging
costs $k$ forward passes per query, whereas \single{} and \swa{} cost one,
so at high query volume the cheapest calibrated option may be a
temperature-scaled single-trajectory model rather than an ensemble. Treat
\swa{} primarily as an accuracy-and-fit tool rather than a reliable shift
detector, consistent
with evidence that OOD failures often persist across training procedures
\citep{hendrycks2017,nguyen2015}. The rule is
bounded by the scope of the evidence, which we summarise in
Sect.~\ref{sec:limitations}.

% ─────────────────────────────── 6 limitations ───────────────────────────────
\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Our evidence covers two small architectures---a 110k-parameter multilayer
perceptron on grayscale digit tasks and, in a post-hoc extension, a
34k-parameter convolutional network on CIFAR-10---both without batch
normalisation or dropout; transformer-based networks, architectures with
normalisation layers (whose interaction with weight averaging raises
separate technical issues), and larger models in general remain untested
here, and the CPU-bound design is the reason. The CIFAR replication uses
one small natural-image task with a single natural-image shift probe at
$32\times32$ resolution and a deliberately modest training budget, so it
demonstrates that the MLP findings are not specific to that regime, not
that they extend to large-scale training. The budget is denominated in
epochs at a fixed learning-rate ceiling and optimizer family rather than
wall-clock or energy; other optimizers or schedules could shift the
crossover points, although not the checkpoint-matched logic of the
\snapshot$-$\swa{} contrast. Inference cost was not part of the matched
budget: prediction averaging costs $k$ forward passes per query, which
matters in deployment (Sect.~\ref{sec:conditions}). Ensemble sizes are
$k \in \{3,5\}$. Our \swa{} variant is checkpoint-matched to
the snapshot ensemble by design; although a dense 40-checkpoint average
did not change the qualitative conclusion, other \swa{} schedules might.
Temperature scaling is the only post-hoc recalibration tested; more
flexible calibrators \citep{zhang2020} could in principle compress the
in-distribution gaps further. The OOD probes are dataset-level shifts
between tasks of the same modality, evaluated with confidence-based
scores; no corruptions, adversarial perturbations or likelihood-based
methods were considered. The OOD multiplicity families of
Sect.~\ref{sec:results-ood} are post-hoc robustness analyses, defined after
the CIFAR results were known, and are reported at several family sizes so
that no conclusion depends on the choice; the pre-specified corrections
cover the NLL and \ts-NLL families only. The CIFAR-10 replication, the budget sweep beyond
$k=3$-at-$B=60$ and all other extensions are post-hoc analyses, labelled
as such throughout, and their statistical corrections do not carry the
pre-registered status of the core setting. Finally, the mechanism analysis
is associational by design; a causal decomposition of the ensemble gain into
member-quality and diversity components would require interventions on
each ingredient separately. One fresh restart of the CIFAR extension
collapsed to near-uniform predictions (Sect.~\ref{sec:results-cifar});
restart-failure rates at other scales and with normalisation layers are
unknown, and the ensemble's tolerance of such failures deserves separate
study.

% ─────────────────────────────── 7 conclusion ───────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared four ways of spending a fixed training budget on the
uncertainty of a classifier, holding compute, data, optimizer and (in part)
checkpoints identical across conditions. Budget-split deep ensembles gave
the best-calibrated in-distribution uncertainty on the primary outcome in
every setting tested---five Fashion-MNIST settings spanning a $4\times$
budget range and two ensemble sizes, a reversed dataset direction, and a
CIFAR-10 replication with a convolutional architecture---an advantage that
grew with the budget and survived per-condition temperature scaling at the
core budget and above, while secondary metrics showed bounded exceptions at
the lowest budget. Single-trajectory strategies captured part of the
benefit on tasks with calibration headroom and none of it on a saturated
task. Prediction averaging improved out-of-distribution detection on
every probe and direction tested, with the largest gains, while weight
averaging of the same checkpoints showed small, task-dependent effects
that ranged from significantly negative to significantly positive. The
paired, seed-replicated, checkpoint-matched template used
here is directly reusable for larger networks, harder shifts and richer
recalibration families, and we release all code and raw records to make
such extensions cheap. In the regimes we measured, the practical answer to
``how should a fixed training budget be spent for calibrated uncertainty,
when the long-trained baseline is overconfident?'' is: across independent
restarts, not along one trajectory.
